# Della Ray Webb — museum biography and separate historical setting

## Biography

Della Ray Webb was born on 13 June 1891 in Colonia Pacheco, Chihuahua, Mexico, to James Wilford Ray and Elsie Margaret Mortensen. Her childhood unfolded among farming colonies linked by extended families, work and repeated moves. Before she was a year old, her family moved into Sonora. They lived at Colonia Oaxaca and later Colonia Morelos, where Della grew into adulthood, married and began raising children of her own.

Her family was large and closely interconnected. James Wilford Ray was married to two sisters, Elsie Margaret and Maria Helena Mortensen. Della was Elsie Margaret's daughter; her half-sister Ada, Maria Helena's daughter, was born only seven weeks later. The two women's households had initially shared a home at Colonia Díaz before another house was built. Della's own marriage would be to one husband, Jonathan Henry Webb, whom she called Henry.

In recollections of her father, Della described a man who was affectionate but firm with his children. His arrival home could announce itself before he appeared: the children recognized the distinctive whistle he made while blowing his nose. One Christmas at Oaxaca, he arrived wearing a Santa Claus mask that frightened the younger children, who had never seen one. The surprise ended with gifts. Della and Ada received little china dishes, with cups so small they fitted over a finger like a thimble. She remembered their delight long afterward.

The household depended on farming along the Bavispe River. Della recalled vegetables and grain, milk, butter and cheese, and potatoes grated to make starch. Older children helped harvest potatoes and peanuts and strip leaves from cane before it was taken to the mill. Juice cooked in large vats became thick syrup. Her father sometimes used the last batch for treats and invited neighbors to share them. Hayrack rides and watermelon gatherings also belonged to her memories of the farms around Morelos.

Farm life included disruption as well as abundance. Della remembered a flood that damaged fields, left sand where crops had grown and changed the river's course. Her family's house was left close to the bank, which they reinforced with wire and branches. Later, she, Chloe and Ada accompanied their father while grain was threshed and cooked for the workers over a campfire, sometimes for a week at a time. The work of feeding a household and a working crew formed part of her life well before she became a mother.

On 16 August 1908, at seventeen, Della married Jonathan Henry Webb in Colonia Morelos. Bishop Charles Lillywhite performed the marriage. Their daughter Thora was born in 1909 and their son Henry Ray in 1911. Della remembered the two little children standing by their grandfather's knees and singing to him. Her early married life remained connected to the Ray household even as she and Henry established their own.

The Mexican Revolution interrupted that life. Della dated their departure for the United States to 12 August 1912. In her handwritten account, she described travel by wagon, taking only necessities and leaving their home and belongings behind. They reached Douglas, Arizona. Her later childhood recollections described government tents arranged in rows, provisions for the displaced families, and visitors walking through the camp to look at them. She remembered the visitors' curiosity as intrusive. The family subsequently spent time in Tucson before their lives took different paths within Arizona.

Della and Henry settled at Thatcher, where Karl Albert was born in January 1914. Their growing family then moved into the White Mountains. Maurice Eugene was born at Pinedale in 1916. Della recalled a move to Show Low in 1917, followed by the birth of Junius Edson in 1918. They returned to Pinedale in 1919, and Gwen, Zola Margaret and James Wilford, known as Jay, were born during that second period there. In November 1926 they moved to Tempe, where Louise was born the following spring. They returned to the mountains in 1929. Their youngest child, Velmer June, was born in April 1930 at Webbville, the family ranch between Show Low and Lakeside.

These ten children—Thora, Henry Ray, Karl Albert, Maurice Eugene, Junius Edson, Gwen, Zola Margaret, James Wilford, Louise and Velmer June—grew up within a household whose needs changed with each move. Della associated her own repeated moves with the example of her parents, whom she regarded as lifelong pioneers. Family ties extended beyond her children. Her father and relatives visited her at Pinedale in 1919. After his death in 1920, her younger brother Waldo lived with relatives and then with Della. Family recollections also describe her helping raise her sister Marguerite and caring for her sister Edna's children, Max and Hattie Sadler, after their mother's death.

Caregiving became one of the most distinctive parts of Della's life. She wrote that she often went into the homes of women who had given birth, nursing the mothers and caring for their newborns. This took many hours alongside the care of her own ten children. On three occasions she delivered a baby before a doctor could arrive. One was her first grandson, Raymond, whose own later tribute recalled that she delivered him at Vernon before the doctor arrived. She also recalled helping care for nearly all her grandchildren when they were born. Her description emphasizes repeated, practical help offered to mothers and babies within the family and surrounding homes.

She made things for the people she loved. Grandchildren remembered her sewing shirts from flour sacks, bottling peas, beans and peaches, and tending a kitchen where a wood-burning stove supplied heat, cooking and hot water. Crochet was her principal hobby, and spare moments often became time for making gifts for children, grandchildren and other relatives. Gardening supplied another kind of care. While the children were growing up, she tended vegetables so that summer meals could include fresh produce. Later, after the children had married and left home, she devoted many hours to flowers.

Grandchildren associated visits with something homemade to eat. Della made candy and cookies and tried to keep bread or cinnamon buns ready because the children asked for them when they arrived. Family gatherings also brought music. She described singers among both her children and grandchildren, and an assortment of instruments that included piano, organ, clarinets, violin, drums, saxophone, trumpets, accordion, trombone, ukulele, guitars and harmonica. Their enjoyment of music gave the gatherings a character she remembered with pleasure.

Della and Henry celebrated fifty years of marriage in 1958. In her later writing, she placed her family at the center of her life, describing the tender care she had given them. Her later years included winter stays in Mesa beside their daughter Louise McDowell. At their Lakeside home near Rainbow Lake, grandchildren remembered bread in the pantry, flowers beneath the kitchen window and Della waving to neighbors from a lawn chair. Lakeside remained her home. She died at McNary General Hospital on 1 December 1969, aged seventy-eight, and was buried on 4 December at Lakeside Cemetery. Henry survived her. Her life joined a childhood in northern Mexico to decades in Arizona, but its most personal traces remain in the work she described: meals prepared, babies tended, gardens kept, gifts crocheted and a home ready for visiting grandchildren.

## Historical setting — separate from Della's biography

**The northern Mexican colonies, 1890–1912.** American farming settlements in Chihuahua and Sonora occupied territory within the longer histories of Mexican communities and Indigenous peoples. Agriculture, schools, local trade and extended-family networks shaped the settlements. During the Mexican Revolution, insecurity and armed conflict led to a major evacuation of American colonists in 1912. Della's retrospective handwritten account dates her departure to 12 August 1912 and describes arrival at Douglas; a separate family-edited childhood recollection adds the tent camp and time in Tucson. Contemporary migration records remain to be correlated.

**The Arizona White Mountains, 1910s–1960s.** The upland settlements around Pinedale, Show Low, Lakeside and McNary developed alongside forestry, logging, sawmilling, grazing and agriculture. Road improvements, changing timber demand and expanded transport links transformed this part of Arizona during Della's adulthood. These local changes provide the backdrop to the family's ranch and sawmill associations, without assuming particular occupations or personal experiences for Della not described in the surviving records.
