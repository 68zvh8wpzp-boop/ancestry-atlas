# Della Ray Webb — museum biography and separate historical setting
**10 October 2026 · DIRECT ANCESTOR · REVIEW DRAFT ONLY · Not installed in the Atlas or approved by the user**

## Biography

Della Ray was born on 13 June 1891 in Colonia Pacheco, Chihuahua, Mexico, the daughter of James Wilford Ray and Elsie Margaret Mortensen. Her family belonged to the network of American-founded farming settlements in northern Mexico, while her mother's family connected her childhood to an earlier journey from Denmark through Utah and Arizona. Her maternal grandfather, Morten Peder Mortensen, had settled in Mexico before Della was born.

During Della's first decade, the Ray family moved between several colonies. From Pacheco they went to Colonia Oaxaca, and in 1900 Elsie Margaret took the children to Colonia Morelos. Morelos had more substantial houses, shops and schools than the family had known in some earlier settlements. Della belonged to an unusually large and interconnected family. Her father, James Wilford Ray, was married to two sisters, Elsie Margaret and Maria Helena Mortensen. The two women's families had first shared a house in Colonia Díaz before separate homes were established. In Colonia Pacheco she and her half-sister Ada—Maria Helena’s daughter, born only seven weeks later—entered the family within the same summer. The sisters grew up within a network of siblings and half-siblings whose lives crossed between these related homes.

At seventeen, Della married Jonathan Henry Webb in Colonia Morelos on 16 August 1908. Their daughter Thora was born there the following year, followed by Henry Ray Webb in 1911. Those were years of increasing instability in northern Mexico. As the Revolution reached the colonies, settlers left for the United States; Della's own family had crossed north by early 1914, when their third child, Karl Albert Webb, was born in Thatcher, Arizona.

The Webbs gradually made their home in Arizona's high country. Maurice Eugene arrived in 1916, Junius Edson in 1918, Gwen in 1919, Zola Margaret in 1923, James Wilford—known as Jay—in 1924, Louise in 1927 and Velmer June in 1930. By 1919 Della was living at Pinedale, where her father and relatives came to visit. The family ties remained practical as well as affectionate: after James Wilford Ray died in 1920, Della's younger brother Waldo stayed with other relatives and then with her. Her own growing household thus remained connected to the younger children of the family she had left in Mexico.

The family's homes were connected to the forests, farms and rural towns of the White Mountains. One of those places became known to the children as Webbville, a ranch between Show Low and Lakeside. Velmer June was born there in April 1930. As the children grew, the family became closely associated with lumber and sawmill work, a defining part of the region's economy. The demands of a large household changed as young children grew older and began building their own homes, but the family connections continued across nearby Arizona towns.

Della lived to see the family move through another generation. Her son Jay married Marion Beulah Brenay in 1941; their children, and the families of Della's other sons and daughters, carried the Webb, Ray and Mortensen connections onward. The settlements in Mexico where Della had spent her childhood lay far from the Arizona homes of her grandchildren, yet both landscapes remained part of the family's history.

Della died on 1 December 1969, according to the Mortensen family history, which places her death at McNary, Arizona. Jonathan outlived her. Her life had crossed the border between Mexico and the United States and spanned a remarkable change in the White Mountains, from small isolated settlements to communities increasingly connected by roads, commerce and industry. At its center remained the ten children she and Jonathan raised and the family they established in Arizona.

## Historical setting — separate from Della's biography

**The northern Mexican colonies, 1890–1912.** American farming settlements in Chihuahua and Sonora occupied territory within the longer histories of Mexican communities and Indigenous peoples. Agriculture, schools, local trade and extended-family networks shaped the settlements. During the Mexican Revolution, insecurity and armed conflict led to a major evacuation of American colonists in 1912. The specific circumstances of Della's own crossing still require a contemporary record; the family's birth chronology places her in Arizona by January 1914.

**The Arizona White Mountains, 1910s–1960s.** The upland settlements around Pinedale, Show Low, Lakeside and McNary developed alongside forestry, logging, sawmilling, grazing and agriculture. Road improvements, changing timber demand and expanded transport links transformed this part of Arizona during Della's adulthood. These local changes provide the backdrop to the family's ranch and sawmill associations, without assuming particular occupations or personal experiences for Della not described in the surviving records.
