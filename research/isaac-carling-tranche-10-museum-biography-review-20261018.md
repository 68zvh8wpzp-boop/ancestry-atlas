## A. Museum biography — proposed Atlas Biography field

**Isaac Van Wagoner Carling (1831–1911)**

Isaac Van Wagoner Carling was born on 30 November 1831 in Ulster County, New York, to John Carling and Emeline Keaton. His family moved westward with the Latter-day Saint migration, passing through Nauvoo, Illinois, where Emeline died during his childhood. His father's later wife, Ann Green Carling, became Isaac's stepmother. Family recollections describe a resourceful boy who repaired a damaged violin, worked with his father on wagons and learned to make things by hand. These stories are preserved in later family writings rather than an examined childhood journal.

At twenty, Isaac joined his father, stepmother and siblings in the overland journey from the Council Bluffs area to Utah. His account of 1852, surviving through a published transcription of his diary, dwells on the practical work of migration: ferrying wagons, repairing equipment, finding feed for animals and coping with storms. He also noticed the graves along the trail and remembered the relief of dancing after difficult days. He reached Salt Lake City ahead of the wagons on 29 September; the wagons arrived the next day. It is an unusually personal record of a long journey otherwise often reduced to a date on a family tree.

The Carlings settled first in Provo and later in Fillmore. A letter dated 19 June 1853, preserved in the family history as a transcription rather than an examined autograph, records Isaac's proposal to Asenath Elizabeth Browning. Their marriage was announced in the *Deseret News*: they married on 17 November 1854 in Ogden City, with Ezra T. Benson officiating. Their daughter Sarah Elizabeth, born in 1856, became an ancestor in the Webb line. Isaac became known within his family as a skilled woodworker who made useful objects as well as ornamental pieces, including furniture and spinning wheels.

During his years in Fillmore, Isaac also established a family with Miriam Elizabeth Hobson while his marriage to Asenath continued, a plural-family arrangement reflected in the surviving family accounts. Miriam and Isaac are remembered as the parents of John Henry, Ellen Alvira, Lydia May and Jesse Hobson. Miriam died in 1869. The exact date and place of the marriage to Miriam remain disputed in later accounts, and the original marriage entry has not been examined. 

In 1876, according to the family history, Isaac relocated with Asenath and their extended household to Orderville in southern Utah, where residents were attempting an unusually comprehensive system of shared labor and production. Later accounts credit his woodworking and painting skills with an important place in the settlement's workshops, and describe him as a cooperative officer and church counselor. The corresponding nineteenth-century board and departmental records have been located, but no page naming Isaac in those offices has yet been read; the exact titles remain family-history claims. The remembered work itself ranged from chairs and cabinets to building trim. Isaac's household also sustained music and gardening. Relatives recalled his violin, seed-growing, drawing lessons and close attention to plants—interests that suggest how craft, family life and curiosity occupied the same space in his daily experience.

Isaac remained in Orderville after the communal system had ended. He died there on 24 May 1911, aged **79**, and family records place his burial in Orderville Cemetery the next day. His legacy is not only the long migration from New York to Utah, but the combination of practical skill, careful observation and artistic interests that later generations associated with the homes and communities he helped build.

## B. Special historical note — Orderville and the United Order (separate context field)

The *United Order* was a Latter-day Saint cooperative economic movement expanded in the 1870s. Orderville, established in **1875** in southern Utah, was one of its most thorough experiments. Families brought labor and productive resources into a shared system; work was organized in departments, and residents made or distributed many of the goods they needed. A common dining hall, carpenter shop, blacksmith shop, commissary and other shared buildings gave the settlement an unusually planned form. The system reduced some household costs and offered opportunities for local manufacturing, but also demanded extensive discipline and blurred boundaries between family, employment and community leadership. Disputes over personal property, competing outside economic opportunities and federal pressure surrounding plural marriage helped weaken communal arrangements, which largely ended in **1885**; some assets continued to be disposed of in later years.

This economic institution was **not identical to plural marriage** or to an individual's church calling. For Isaac, the communal workshops provide a well-documented setting for work attributed to him by descendants, but neither the town's existence nor the catalog of its board minutes independently proves a particular job title.

## C. Optional special historical note — 1852 Carling/Webb trail intersection

Isaac's published diary entry for **1 August 1852** says the Miller party passed “Curtis' Company.” Church History places the five-year-old future son-in-law **Edward Milo Webb Jr.** in Uriah Curtis's company; that boy's father, Edward Milo Webb Sr., had died on **31 July 1852**. This is a company-level convergence, **not proof** of an encounter, sighting, or knowledge of the Webb family's bereavement. The two migration stories were brought into the same direct line only decades later. Keep as a special research/historical note, **not** an invented scene in Isaac's biography.
