Edward Milo Webb Jr., known in his family as Eddie, was born on 8 March 1847 at Linden in northwestern Missouri. His parents were Edward Milo Webb and Caroline Amelia Owens. He belonged to a family already accustomed to movement between the settlements of Illinois and Missouri. His childhood would carry him much farther, across the plains to Utah, and his adult life would take him into the schools and settlements of Arizona and northern Mexico.

In 1852, when Edward was five, his family traveled west with the Uriah Curtis Company. His father died near the Platte River on 31 July, aged thirty-six, leaving Caroline to continue with the children. Edward’s childhood crossing became one of the family’s enduring memories. A later account describes him walking much of the distance and helping drive livestock. Another recalls his becoming absorbed in a river crossing and being temporarily left behind. These memories preserve the journey from a small child’s perspective: animals to tend, unfamiliar surroundings, and a family suddenly deprived of its father.

Caroline brought the children to Utah and subsequently made a home in Fillmore. Edward’s daughter Irene later described her grandmother as his first teacher. In that account, early work limited his schooling, but his mother taught him the beginnings of reading and learning. He continued studying for himself and later received instruction from C. H. Riggs and John R. Park. His education developed through a mixture of home teaching, independent study, and periods of formal instruction, rather than a simple uninterrupted school career.

Two census entries place a thirteen-year-old Edward in Fillmore in July 1860. One names him as Edward M. Webb in an Owens household; the other places Edward M. among Caroline’s household. Both report Missouri as his birthplace. They offer glimpses of the extended family network around him after his father’s death, although the schedules do not explain why he appears twice.

Teaching became central to Edward’s working life. On 24 June 1870 the Fillmore census recorded E. M. Webb, twenty-three, as a schoolteacher. Ellen Webb, sixteen and born in England, appears in the same household group. Edward had married Ellen Ashman on 6 June that year. Their marriage began a family that would grow through the years of territorial settlement, repeated moves, and the demands of supporting children in communities where money and institutions were often scarce.

Edward entered plural marriage with Sarah Elizabeth Carling on 10 April 1873 and married Charlotte Martha Maxwell on 13 September 1878. These were overlapping marriages. Ellen, Sarah, and Charlotte each had a household and children, and the three families remained closely connected. The marriages formed part of the nineteenth-century Latter-day Saint practice of plural marriage and shaped the responsibilities, domestic arrangements, and public pressures surrounding the family.

Edward and Ellen’s fourteen children were Abigail Ann, Edward Milo, Ellen Amelia, Caroline Amanda, Francis John, Harriet Alice, Albert Dorus, Clarence Estelvin, Irene Adell, Cordelia Alathea, Harvey Nelson, Lillian Gertrude, Wayne Llewellyn, and Lois.

Edward and Sarah’s twelve children were Rachel Asenath, Ether B., Owen Adelbert, Catherine Alvira, Isaac Clark, Abraham Dewitt, Jonathan Henry, Thomas Howard, Edson Burr, Laura Martha, Eliza May, and Sydney Jesse. Jonathan Henry carried this branch of the Webb family into the next generation.

Edward and Charlotte’s five children were Lucretia Annetta, William Marcellus, James Levi, Ruth Estelle, and Julia Isabelle. Together the three families numbered thirty-one children, including children who died young. This was a family spread across three maternal households, joined by shared work, visiting, celebrations, religious observance, and the relationships among the children.

By 1880 Edward was living in Orderville, Utah Territory. The census calls him a schoolteacher and explicitly identifies Ellen as his wife, with five children in their household. Sarah and two children appear in the preceding separate household entry. Orderville’s communal economy pooled property and organized work for the settlement. Family histories also describe Edward keeping accounts and helping administer the community. Teaching and bookkeeping drew on the same abilities: literacy, arithmetic, orderly records, and the management of other people’s practical needs.

The family subsequently moved into northeastern Arizona. Edward worked in education, trade, and community leadership around Woodruff and Snowflake. A letter written to Karl G. Maeser on 19 October 1889 states that Brother Webb had been acting as principal of the Snowflake academy but was needed as a bishop; it proposed Levi Savage as his replacement. The letter places the change within the practical staffing problems of a small regional school system. Edward’s educational and religious responsibilities competed for the same limited time.

Irene remembered a man whose working knowledge extended beyond the classroom. Her account names surveying, brickmaking, and farming among his occupations and says declining health eventually made prolonged indoor teaching difficult. He adapted by undertaking outdoor work. The breadth of those occupations reflects the conditions of small settlements, where a household’s livelihood might depend on several trades rather than a single permanent position.

The family later lived in the Mormon colonies of northern Mexico. Charlotte continued teaching there. Estelle described Edward surveying watercourses and making bricks with his sons, adapting his skills to the practical needs of new communities. Her account also describes an ambition to develop electric power from irrigation water, a plan interrupted by revolution. His work thus reached beyond a classroom into the physical systems that allowed a settlement to function. Edward’s daughter Estelle remembered this period through the life of the three homes. Children moved freely among them, ate at one another’s tables, and joined in outings and celebrations. Ellen was remembered for English cooking, Sarah for bread and practical household skills, and Charlotte for entertaining and organizing excursions. These are a daughter’s affectionate recollections of family life, including her recognition that disagreements could occur beyond the children’s hearing.

Music gave the large family a common language. Estelle recalled singing around an organ, songs composed by her father, and numerous children playing instruments. The children staged plays for their relatives and sometimes the neighbors. Edward combined firm expectations with a taste for jokes and an interest in poetry and music. His daughter remembered that he disliked treating the children as half-siblings: they belonged to one family, despite having different mothers.

The household also endured illness and bereavement. Estelle recalled young Wayne’s final illness in Garcia, Mexico, and Charlotte helping Ellen and Edward care for him. The family’s closeness did not eliminate loss; it provided additional hands for nursing and people with whom to share it. The deaths of children formed part of the family’s experience alongside the more frequently remembered singing, meals, and performances.

The Mexican Revolution disrupted the colonies, and the family returned to the United States in 1912. Edward was sixty-five. The upheaval interrupted homes and livelihoods built over years and brought him back into an extended family increasingly distributed across Arizona and Utah. His later years were affected by poor health, while his children and grandchildren established households of their own.

Edward died in Murray, Utah, on 11 September 1921, aged seventy-four years, six months, and three days. His death certificate names Ellen as his wife, Hattie Webb as informant, and cancer of the stomach as the cause of death. He was buried at Holladay Cemetery on 12 September. His life had begun before the great overland migration and ended after territorial settlements had become established towns. He left a large family, a long record of teaching and practical work, and memories of a father whose books, music, humor, and expectations helped connect three households.
