## Biography

Caroline Amelia Owens was born on **9 July 1821**, according to the Church History Biographical Database, in **Warren, Ohio**, the daughter of James Clark Owens and Abigail Cordelia Burr. Her early years unfolded as her family moved westward through the expanding settlements of Ohio and Missouri. Family recollections place her among those who experienced the upheaval surrounding the expulsion of Latter-day Saint settlers from Missouri in 1838, before the family reached Illinois.

At **18**, Caroline married **Edward Milo Webb** on **21 December 1839**. They established their household amid the changing settlements of the Mississippi Valley. A particularly tangible record of Caroline survives from **12 May 1842**, when the handwritten minutes of the Nauvoo Relief Society listed **“Amelia C. Webb”** among the women admitted to membership. Editors of the Joseph Smith Papers identify her as Caroline Amelia Webb. The entry places her in the young community at about twenty years old, rather than leaving her presence to later family memories alone.

By **1850**, the Webbs were living in Missouri. Two years later, Caroline, Edward and their four surviving young children—**Cordelia, Marcellus, Edward Jr. and Caroline**—joined **Uriah Curtis’s wagon company** for the journey to Utah. Edward’s younger sister **Mary Jane Webb** traveled with them. Family recollections describe the task of moving children, livestock and possessions across the plains. On the route, the company was struck by cholera. Edward reportedly died near the **Platte River on 31 July 1852**, aged **36**, leaving Caroline, then **31**, to complete the journey with the children. Another company member, Lucy Benson Wilson, independently recalled multiple deaths during the outbreak. The exact place of Edward’s burial has not been established.

Caroline arrived in Utah pregnant. Her youngest son, **Francis Adelbert (“Dell”) Webb**, was born on **20 March 1853** in the Salt Lake Valley, according to family records. After an initial period near Cottonwood, she settled at **Fillmore**, where family accounts describe her using practical skills to support the household: sewing and tailoring, teaching, gardening and making dairy products. These recollections portray her not simply as a widow on the trail, but as a worker rebuilding a home and livelihood in a developing community.

In **1856**, Caroline married **Alexander McRae**, who was already married to **Eunice Fitzgerald McRae**. Her marriage was therefore part of a **plural household**. Caroline and Alexander had two children, **Albert** and **Julia Estella McRae**. A later McRae family biography describes difficulties between the households and indicates that Caroline and Alexander were living separately by about 1858. The reported separation is supported by family history, although no original divorce decree has been examined.

Caroline remained associated with Fillmore for much of the rest of her life. A letter attributed to her and dated **2 June 1889**, preserved in a family transcription, describes the everyday texture of her later years: relatives’ work, strawberries, household arrangements and concern for her son's family. Other recollections remember her needlework and painting. Together they offer a more personal portrait of a woman whose responsibilities changed considerably across five decades.

She died in **Fillmore on 1 September 1895**, aged **74**, and was buried there on **3 September**. A descendant-preserved funeral account describes the Fillmore band, women of the Relief Society and family members accompanying her procession through the town. Caroline’s life connected the unsettled American frontier of her childhood with a mature Utah community that she had helped sustain through family care, skilled work and long residence.

## Local and regional historical context

Caroline grew up in Ohio’s **Western Reserve**, a region shaped by migration from the northeastern states, expanding agriculture and new towns. During the 1830s, families moved between Ohio, Missouri and Illinois in search of land, livelihoods and communities; conflicts over settlement and religion contributed to violence and expulsions, particularly in Missouri in 1838.

Travel to Utah in **1852** required families to cross long stretches of the Great Plains with limited medical help or dependable supplies. Cholera could spread through wagon camps, and accounts from the Curtis company describe several deaths. **Fillmore**, founded in the early territorial period and briefly Utah’s capital, depended on local agriculture, animal husbandry and trades. Sewing, teaching, preserving food and maintaining extended-family networks were economically important activities for women supporting frontier households. Political and religious institutions also shaped marriage and family arrangements, including the plural households that became part of Caroline’s later life.
