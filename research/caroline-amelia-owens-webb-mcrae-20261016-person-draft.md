## Museum biography — DRAFT FOR LATER EDITORIAL APPROVAL (~640 words)

Caroline Amelia Owens was born in the Western Reserve of northeastern Ohio, probably on **9 July 1821** at Warren in Trumbull County, although some family transcriptions instead give **6 July** or a different Ohio birthplace. She grew up amid the migrations and religious experimentation that carried her family through Ohio and Missouri during the 1830s. She later described witnessing the violence and forced displacement of residents at Far West, Missouri. In her own account, as reproduced in a family history, the experience was not an abstract historical event but the loss of security and the difficulty of finding shelter after expulsion. The original handwriting has not yet been examined.

At **18**, Caroline married **Edward Milo Webb** on **21 December 1839**, after their families had reached Illinois. During the next decade they lived at several settlements, including Nauvoo and later Missouri, while Edward traveled to Michigan and other places for church work. She managed the demands of a growing household as the family prepared to move west. An original 1850 Missouri census indexed under the child Caroline/Edmond Webb identifies their household, providing a firmer contemporaneous anchor than later recollections.

In 1852, aged **30**, Caroline joined the **Uriah Curtis emigrant company** with Edward and four named young children: **Cordelia, Marcellus, Edward Jr., and Caroline**. Edward's younger sister Mary Jane Webb, aged 21, also traveled in their company. A contemporary *Deseret News* roster lists Edward M. Webb with a wife and five children; that shorthand does not distinguish the sister from their biological children. Caroline's family later remembered oxen, two wagons, cows and sheep as part of the household's equipment. The company was struck by fatal illness on the trail. Edward reportedly died of cholera near the Platte River on **31 July 1852**, aged **36**. A fellow traveler, Lucy Benson Wilson, independently recalled multiple cholera deaths in the Curtis company, though she did not name Edward or identify his grave. The exact burial site is unknown.

Caroline continued to Utah as a young widow with four small children and another child expected. Her youngest son, **Francis Adelbert ("Dell") Webb**, was reportedly born in the Salt Lake Valley on **20 March 1853**, almost eight months after his father's death. After an initial stay around Cottonwood, she moved her family to **Fillmore**, where the newly forming settlement offered a place to begin again. Family reminiscences describe her earning money through **tailoring and sewing**, as well as making butter and cheese, cultivating a garden and teaching school. A reported **25 August 1855** property-consecration schedule lists acreage, animals and household possessions with a stated value of **$362**. Its original recorded deed has not yet been located; nor should the deed be mischaracterized as proven loss of all those possessions, because the contemporaneous consecration movement largely involved symbolic transfers rather than actual surrender of property.

In approximately **1855–1856**, Caroline became a wife of **Alexander McRae**, a territorial official who was already married to **Eunice Fitzgerald McRae**. Contemporary institutional records confirm Caroline's later surname and marriage in **1856**; a separately compiled biography describes a sealing on **17 May 1856** and identifies **Brigham Young** as officiant, though the actual sealing-register entry remains to be inspected. This was described as a **plural marriage** in family accounts, and it brought Caroline into a complicated household. She and Alexander had two children, **Albert McRae** (1856) and **Julia Estella McRae** (1858), already represented in her FamilySearch household. The same biography suggests Caroline and Alexander were living separately by around 1858 and relays a claim of divorce related to financial support; no court or church dissolution original has yet been found. This should be presented as a reported separation, not a certified divorce.

Caroline's later years were centered in the Millard County communities she helped develop. Family recollections describe her as a skilled needleworker and painter, a careful reader and an engaging conversationalist. A **2 June 1889** letter attributed to her survives in an online family transcription, addressed to Dell and his wife Belle. Instead of grand pronouncements, it discusses the household's strawberries, relatives' health and work, and the comfort of living with family. The text gives a rare opportunity to hear her everyday concerns in her own voice, though the original letter has not yet been inspected.

Caroline died at **Fillmore on 1 September 1895**, aged **74**. A detailed account of her funeral two days later—preserved in a descendant transcription and attributed at its close to clerk **Nelson S. Bishop**—describes a procession including the **Fillmore band**, Relief Society women, the hearse, and carriages carrying relatives and friends. It reports a full meetinghouse. The original clerk's record or newspaper notice has not yet been recovered. Caroline's life had stretched from Ohio's early-settlement era through Missouri displacement, the deadly emigrant crossing and decades of practical work in Utah. The surviving letters and memories suggest that her contribution was not confined to the history of the men she married: she built the means for her family to endure.

## Separate historical context — DRAFT (~170 words)

Northeastern Ohio's **Western Reserve** connected eastern migration routes with young towns and farms in the 1820s–1830s. Religious movements and westward land opportunities drew mobile families into Missouri and Illinois, where tension over settlement, property, religion and politics sometimes became violent. In 1838, members of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints were forcibly expelled from Missouri; the Webb/Owens household later joined the Nauvoo settlement in Illinois.

The overland route from Iowa to Utah in **1852** crossed a dangerous plains environment. Cholera moved through emigrant companies, and multiple deaths occurred in the Curtis company itself. A surviving diary from a different company places Curtis’s train near the Platte river forks by early August, but cannot fix Edward Webb’s exact death or grave. After the crossing, **Fillmore** was one of the earliest government and settlement centers in Utah Territory. Money, manufactured goods and hired labor were scarce. Women commonly relied on gardens, dairy work, sewing, teaching and extended kinship networks to sustain households. The **1850s consecration-deed movement** recorded proposed conveyances of property to church trustees, but surviving forms do not necessarily show actual physical transfer or dispossession.
