EDWARD MILO WEBB JR. - page 7

In 1893 father and his brother, Francis Adelbert, attended the dedication of the
Salt Lake Temple. Father also visited the World's Fair in Chicago.
After an interuption of twelve years, father and family undertook to finish their
long delayed journey to Mexico. George Teasdale of the Council of the Twelve had charge
of the colonies in Mexico and had offered father a position at the Colonia Juarez Academy.
For weeks the mothers were busy baking box after box of crackers and cookies and other
things to eat on the way and the children were sing-songing, "Minnie, minnie, miny, mo
on the road to Mexico, to Mexico we'll go or bust, when we start just see the dust".
The caravan left Woodruff June 28, 1891. There were five wagons in the train; the
"chuck" wagon which Clark drove, John Dewitt's wagon, then Mother's, Aunt Sarah's and
Aunt Lottie's. This was a glorious trip for the children at least for there were so many
interesting things for them along the way; the camp fires at night, exploring the camp-
ing grounds, watching bread being cooked in bake ovens, gathering "buffalo chips" for the
fire when wood wasn't available, gathering flowers, wild grass nuts and pine gum and, most
interesting, the group singing in the evenings while John DeWitt accompanied them on the
guitar. During the journey a horse was stolen but was replaced with "Stockings" the
little pony Milo had given to Dorus. We had to "lay over" several weeks before "crossing
the line" while waiting for the Mexicans "manyana" to come when they would let us
through the custom house; but nothing else of conquence happened that I can remember.
The Juarez Stake of Zion at that time consisted of Latter-day Saints residing in
eight wards. Juarez, Dublan, Diaz, Garcia, Pacheco and Chuchupi in the state of Chua-
hushua and Oaxaca in Sonora. Father first settled in the colony of Dublan. The company
arrived after a six weeks journey and had been there only about one week when John and
Carrie DeWitt's son, Dee, was born. Two months later father's and mother's youngest child
Lois, was born.
Never very husky and always working beyond his strength, father's years of sedentary
life had now ruined his health; consequently he had to give up school teaching for rec-
overy was to be had only in outdoor living. Much of the time father was quite ill, yet
he planned and supervised the boy's work who quarried rocks and made the brick for many
of the big houses in Dublan. Among them was one for L. Paul Cardon,his daughter, Katie,
later married Harvey. After the brick kilns were made father and sons built houses in
town for the families. They lived there two or three years then John DeWitt sold his
home and went back, again, to Woodruff, Arizona. Father sold his homes and moved his
families to Garcia, one of the mountain colonies. Colonia Garcia is situated about fifty
miles from Dublan in the heart of the Sierra Madre Mountains. The first settlers had been
in Garcia only a short time when the Webbs settled there on a farm just out of town in
a little round valley. Here they raised crops and on a steep bank of a wash, made tar
to sell by burning pitch pine logs. It was in Garcia that little, sweet-spirited Wayne
died and where Clark married Hilda Cluff. Frank had married Edith Farr but he lived in
Dublan.
In 1904 father moved Aunt Sarah's and Aunt Lottie's families to Morelos where colon-
ies were pioneering a new settlement. When Hattie returned to Dublan from Woodruff where
she had been teaching school, father moved mother's family to Morelos. We traveled in a
covered wagon drawn by two small mules, Rat and Mouse.
