EDWARD MILO WEBB JR. - page 8

There were nine thousand acres of land in Morelos. President Ivins purchased it
for the Colonization Company and sold it to the colonists. Those of our family who
bought land were Owen, Clark, Dorus, Lee, Henry, Burr, Harvey, Hattie and our father.
In 1900 Morelos was covered with a matted forest of mesquite trees, catclaw, chaparral,
cacti and other vegetation. These all had to be cut down and the land cleared before
crops could be planted. Soil in Morelos was deep, very fertile and yielded abundantly.
Besides melons, sweet potatoes and all garden vegetables, peanuts, wheat and other grains
were grown and marketed in Douglas, Arizona. To water the land and for culinary purposes
settlers tapped the Bavispe River at the head of the canyon and constructed a big canal
from there to town. The banks of this river were fringed with willows, and cottonwood
trees. The clear water in it was full of fish. The catfish which sometimes measured
two feet long people speared with pitch forks. In pioneering this country our parents
felt the lack of comforts and suffered many trials; but Morelos days were happy ones for
the young people as they were so full of interesting experiences.
Father's health, now, was very poor, especially after a serious illness of blood
poisoning and a breaking out of boils. This left his heart very weak and he was told
by a doctor in Douglas, who examined him, that it was so bad he could not live much
longer. However, by using wisdom and watching himself carefully he was finally able to
get around though he had to lie down much of the time. In spite of ill health father
persisted in working to the limit of his strength. He coached the children who were stud-
ing to teach school, studied shorthand, worked all he could on the farm, supervised the
making of brick, took his children on trips to the mountains and to Douglas to attend to
interesting and educational things, did his church duties, worked up all his assessments
and when he was too weak to do more strenuous work, he improved his mind by reading and
studying.
Under President Diaz the Mormons in Mexico were treated kindly and were encouraged
to build up the country. Madero, too, treated them as well as possible under the cir-
cumstances. But the local authorities were arrogant, domineering, abusive and very
oppressive. They ignored, entirely, the rights and privileges of the American citizens
and gave them no protection from the rebels. Before the revolution in 1911 the colonists
were prosperous and happy. In Morelos, particularly, peoples homes were more comfortable
and people dressed better; for over the years their crops had been abundant. This year
fifty thousand bushels of wheat were raised. The three hundred acres of alfalfa planted
produced a very good yield and the peanut crop was splended. The next year, 1912, these
flourishing Mormon colonies became a prey to contending military forces and at last the
people were forced to leave their all and flee, penniless, to the United States for
safety.
When the colonists escaped from Mexico they fled to cities on the border most con-
vient to them. Morelos refugees went to Douglas, Arizona; the Diaz refugees to Hachita,
New Mexico and the other colonists to El Paso, Texas. The U.S. Government furnished the
refugees with some flour and salt bacon, etc and some soldiers' tents. These were pitched
outside the towns and every day spectators would gather round the camps and watch
us as if we were strange animals.
