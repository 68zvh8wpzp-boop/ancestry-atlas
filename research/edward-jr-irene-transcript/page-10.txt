EDWARD MILO WEBB JR. - 9

Jacob Huber in his record of the exodus from Morelos wrote: "on Sept. 30, 1912
a dozen of the men colonists ventured out of Douglas to ascertain, if possible, to the
amount of damage done to the posessions of the colonists. On arriving at the colony
they found that every house had been looted and everything of value taken. Sewing
machines and furniture ruthlessly smashed up and lay around as debris while house
organs which was to be found in almost every home were heaps of kindling wood .
Carcasses of dead animals lay about the street, doors and windows were smashed in,
stores were gutted and the contents strewn everywhere about while here and there a cash
register or some other modern appliance gave evidence of the hand of prejudice, de-
stroying ignorance".
The colonists believed that their exodus was only temporary; but they learned to
their sorrow that they could not return to their homes. Since the United States
Government furnished them with transportation on the railroads to any point in the
union they wished to go, the refugees scattered and went wherever they could make a
living best for their families. Owen, Henry, Dorus, Clark, Lee and Cordelia were now
married. They with father's and some other refugee families located in Tucson, Arizona
where Frank was living.
When the Mexico refugees arrived in Tucson, a Tucson Farms Company had a large
tract of land at Jaynes Station to be cleared of mesquite trees, leveled and put into
shape for cultivation. The refugees secured the contract for this work and it took
them a year to do it. A branch of the church was organized and L. Paul Cardon served
as branch president. After the land was cleared some of the families moved away to
other cities. The remaining families established themselves at Binghamton, a little
community in the suburbs of Tucson where a few families of Latter-day Saints lived.
Father, now, had arrived at the age when life should have been easier for him - he was
not physically able to do hard work - but his families were without homes and might soon
be destitute therefore, he uncomplainingly and cheerfully began anew to make them com-
fortable. In a few years with the help of his children, father succeeded in getting
good homes for all. He built houses and clear land on homesteads around Pinedale,
Arizona for Aunt Sarah and Aunt Lottie on which they "proved up" and got the deeds.
Mother had a home built in Binghamton where her sons Frank, Dorus and Harvey owned a
dairy. Hattie was principal of Binghamton district school and Irene taught sewing in the
Arizona State School for the Deaf and Blind. Later mother had a home built in Snowflake.
Still later mother had a home in Salt Lake City where she lived until her death.
In his last few years father, as devoted to his grandchildren as he had been to his
own children, went back and forth among the families always helping and encouraging them
in every way he could. And to the last father's constant desire for knowledge never left
him. He studied and kept in touch with the world's progress just as long as he could
see to read.
On June 6, 1920, father and mother, Ellen Ashman Webb, celebrated their fiftieth
wedding anniversary. In their honor, a program was given in the Binghamton Ward Chapel.
Many friends and relatives were present. This year father made a trip to Utah and Idaho
to interest relatives in genealogy work. The result was: a Webb Surname Organization.
Father died 11 September 1921 at Murray, Utah and was buried at Holladay, Utah
