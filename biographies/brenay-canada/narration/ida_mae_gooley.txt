Ida Mae Gooley was born on July 11, 1872, in Alpena, Michigan, the daughter of John Peter Gooley and Mary Ann Dennis. Her father had been born in Quebec and her mother in Ontario, so the Canadian side of Ida’s story was already woven into an American household on Lake Huron.

One day after Ida’s birth, fire swept through Alpena. Flames destroyed homes, businesses and mills across much of the young lumber town. Ida could not remember the disaster, but she entered life in a community forced almost immediately to rebuild around her. A surviving photograph of Alpena after the fire records shattered buildings and streets, although no Gooley property or family member is identified.

Alpena’s recovery followed the rhythms of the Great Lakes. Timber came out of the forests, ships entered Thunder Bay, and mills, railways and workshops drew families into a growing industrial town. Michigan, Ontario and Quebec were connected by water, work and kinship more closely than an international boundary might suggest. Later photographs of Alpena’s lumber yards and rebuilt commercial blocks show the industrial town that rose around Ida’s generation.

As a young woman, Ida married Charles Godfrey Brenay, a German immigrant whose name also appeared as Gottfried Brunne. Their household brought together three migrations: Charles’s journey from Germany, John Peter’s movement from Quebec and Mary Ann’s Ontario beginnings.

By the 1890s, Ida and Charles were living in Ontario. Their son Charles Albert was born at Arnstein in 1897. Arnstein placed the family in a northern landscape of forest settlements, small farms and timber connections reaching toward Georgian Bay.

At the opening of the twentieth century, the household was still in Ontario. Around 1903, the family crossed into Michigan and settled again in Alpena. Railways and Great Lakes transportation had made movement between Ontario and Michigan increasingly practical. A period Ontario station photograph provides context for the rail network of that borderland, without identifying the Brenays’ route or train.

Ida’s children grew up carrying identities that did not fit neatly on one side of the border. Charles Albert was Canadian-born, raised partly in Michigan and still identified as a Canadian citizen or subject when he registered for the American draft in 1918.

In 1925, Charles Albert and his young family left Michigan for Arizona, extending the family’s movement far beyond the Great Lakes. Ida lived until 1932, long enough to see that next generation begin a western life during an era of automobiles, long-distance roads and rapid national change.

Ida Mae’s life joined Quebec ancestry, an Ontario household and an Alpena birthplace rebuilt from fire. Through her, the Gooley and Brenay families became one cross-border line whose descendants eventually carried their Canadian history into the American Southwest.
