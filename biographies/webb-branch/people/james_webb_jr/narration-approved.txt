James Webb Jr. was born on March 21, 1777, according to later family histories. Connecticut anchors his early life, with East Hampton or Chatham as the strongest current locality. He entered the world while the American Revolution was still underway and before the United States Constitution had been written.

The surviving record is incomplete. The East Hampton Congregational register has a gap across the period in which James’s baptism might have appeared. That absence does not prove that he was born elsewhere or belonged to another family. A missing record is not the same thing as a negative record.

By adulthood, James had married Hannah Griswold, born in Connecticut in 1790. Their children included Edward Milo Webb, born in Hanover, New York, in 1815. The family’s movement from Connecticut into western New York followed a major stream of post-Revolution migration as new roads, land markets and settlements opened beyond New England.

Later, members of the household moved again through the rapidly changing communities of the early nineteenth century. The supported Webb line continues from James and Hannah to Edward Milo Webb Sr., then through Missouri, Utah Territory and Arizona. That descendant chain is far better documented than the generation immediately above James.

Published family histories identify James’s parents as another James Webb and a woman named Elizabeth Douglas. The father’s name is strongly supported in the compiled tradition, but no decisive town, land or probate record has yet been found. The mother’s identity presents a sharper problem: records and compiled trees appear to combine two different women named Elizabeth Douglas, producing conflicting dates and identities.

For that reason, James’s generation marks the boundary of the supported Webb story. James Webb Sr. and Elizabeth Douglas remain a parentage problem rather than a doorway to a finished colonial or English pedigree. Gideon Webb, Edith Bates and the proposed older line remain provisional or frontier leads rather than established ancestors.

The next proof must come from a locality-wide study of East Hampton and Chatham town and church material, land transactions, probate files, households, siblings and associates. One record may not settle the question by itself; the answer may emerge only when several records point to the same family.

James Webb Jr. is therefore both an ancestor and the present edge of the supported Webb story. The line reaches him with substantial evidence. Beyond him, the light deliberately dims—not because the family necessarily ends there, but because responsible history stops where identity has not yet been proved.
