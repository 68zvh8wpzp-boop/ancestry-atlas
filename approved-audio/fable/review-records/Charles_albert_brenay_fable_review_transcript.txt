Charles Albert Brenay was born on March 29, 1897, in Arnstein, Ontario, the son of Charles Godfrey Brenay and Ida Mae Gooley. Arnstein lay in the forested Parry Sound District, where small farms and settlements were linked to the wider Great Lakes economy by rough roads, railways and the timber trade. Canada was still part of the British Empire under Queen Victoria.

When Charles was a young boy, the family moved from Ontario to Alpena, Michigan. Alpena was a Lake Huron lumber and shipping town, crowded with mills, docks, rail lines and families moving around the Great Lakes. By 1910, the Brenay household was established there.

In June 1918, as the First World War drew toward its final months, Charles registered for the draft in Alpena. He signed the card as Albert Brenay and identified Canada as the country whose citizen or subject he remained. The war was transforming borders and national identities, but his Canadian origin was still part of his public identity.

Charles married Marian Beulah Skinner, and their family began in Michigan. Their daughter Marion was born in Saginaw in 1923. Two years later, Charles and Marian loaded four small children and everything they could carry onto an old one-ton Ford truck covered with canvas and headed west.

The journey took thirty days. Their route ran through Chicago, Council Bluffs, Omaha, Cheyenne, Salt Lake City, St. George, Las Vegas and Needles, where the temperature reached 126 degrees. They crossed the Colorado River by ferry at Parker and reached Mesa on July 17, 1925. Most of the way was gravel, dirt or little more than a trail. A surviving period photograph—not of the Brenays—shows the kind of canvas-covered truck in which families undertook such journeys before dependable highways.

The family arrived as automobiles were changing migration but before paved highways made long-distance travel dependable. Charles kept searching for land, work and a way to support a growing household. He built modest homes, repaired machinery, operated shoe shops and expected the older children to work beside the family. Archival photographs of western children, rough roads and improvised travel preserve the material world surrounding the family’s search for stability.

During the Depression, free land drew them to a homestead near Buckeye, but desert conditions and family losses ended the attempt. A land trade and promised sawmill work took them to Nutrioso; when the arrangement failed, they returned to Mesa. The hope of raising their own food led next to Blanding, Utah, where dry farming also failed. A move to Salt Lake City brought continued hardship, and the family again returned to Arizona.

In 1940, their son Ammon suffered repeated pneumonia. A doctor recommended Arizona’s pine country, and a farm trade brought the family from Mesa to Lakeside. The move joined health, land and opportunity, placing them in a small mountain community connected to farming and timber work. A period Arizona school-transport photograph provides context for the isolated mountain communities into which the family moved; the people shown are not the Brenays.

By the time Charles died in 1983, his life had crossed an international border, two world wars, the Great Depression and the transformation from wagon roads to modern highways. Through every change ran the same determined search for land, useful work and a more secure life for his family.
