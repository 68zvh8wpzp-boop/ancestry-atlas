WEBB FAMILY REUNION
June 1998

HENRY AND DELLA WEBB

By
Ardell and Marjorie Ellsworth

We live in a special time and in a very special place. There are those who say we
are a chosen people living in a choice land — the land of America.

Each one of us has a story to tell. The things we do are important to us, and when

the last history book will close, our story will be important to those who follow after
us.

Tonight, our story is a small part of the lives of Henry and Della Webb. They
were and are great people in the lives of all who have gathered here tonight. To
know them better helps each of us to understand who we are, where we came from
and why we are here. We know of their honesty, their hard work, how they loved
their family and of their love and trust in their God.

Come with us to Linden, Missouri to begin our story. This is where Henry’s
father, Edward Milo, was born to Edward Milo Webb Sr. and Caroline Amelia
Owens on March 8, 1847. They called him Eddie for short, as he began his life in
that humble home on the flat lands of Missouri. The family was very poor, but this
did not deter them from listening to the message of Joseph Smith concerning a new
book- a book that would lead us to eternal life and exaltation. The book was called
the Book of Mormon. The Webb family accepted its teachings and were baptized
members of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter Day Saints.

With Brigham Young as the new president and prophet of the church, it became
evident that the members must seek a new home where they could worship. There
was great persecution of the saints at that time, and the prophet told them to move
west to the Rocky Mountains.

The Webb family made the decision to join the others and cross the plains in
search of religious freedom. They prepared themselves the best they could, took
their meager belongings and joined the others in the migration west. The trip was
difficult, and it tried the very souls of those who put religious freedom before their
comforts.

Page 2

Eddie was only five years old when the family began the trek across the plains.
At that age, he was too small to help his parents. Somewhere on the trail, his father
died and it became a very trying time for his young mother to carry on.

It was known that he served in the Blackhawk War as a soldier, but there is no
other record of his youth.

During this time, many members of the Mormon Church were asked by leaders
of the church to live plural marriage. Although the church and leaders approved it,
not all practiced the principle.

On June 6, 1870 Edward Milo was married to his first wife, Ellen Ashman.
They had fourteen children from 1870-1898. On April 10, 1873, Edward Milo was
married to his second wife, Sarah Elizabeth Carling, the mother of Jonathan Henry.
This union brought twelve children.

In 1876, the Webb family moved to Orderville, Utah to live the United Order, as
directed by the church leaders at that time. Two years later, Edward would marry
his third wife, Charlotte Martha Maxwell, Aunt Lottie, on September 13, 1878. She
would give birth to five children.

After nine years in Orderville, the church authorities disbanded the small Utah
town in 1885. They asked the families to move to other settlements. The next year,
the Webb family gathered their belongings and made preparations for the move to
Mexico, where they would settle the country and establish the church. They traveled
the dusty and muddy road to the South. They traveled the Honeymoon Trail, across
the deep canyons, through the pine tangled plateaus and barren deserts to the mighty
Colorado River.

John D. Lee operated a ferry across the Colorado River for the travelers. He
called his home the Lonely Dell. He had many personal problems but also a great
desire to serve his fellow men. This was his way to show his love to those who were
called to be pioneers.

The Webb family crossed the Colorado River and made their way to the valley
along the Little Colorado Basin in northeast Arizona. They arrived in the small
town of Woodruff, Arizona on May 2, 1886. They planned a short rest but their visit
lasted a lot longer. One month later, Edward Milo’s wife Sarah, gave birth to a new

Page 3

son. His name would be Jonathan Henry. It was on June 21, 1886 that this son was
born in the small town in the sandstone desert.

To the south of Woodruff was another town settled by Mormon pioneers. They
had established a church school in the town of Snowflake which would be called
“The Academy”. Students would come from all over to get more education. They
had no teachers or a principal to run the school. Edward and his wife Charlotte
were hired to teach and run the school.

Edward Milo had very little formal schooling. One summer when he was 17
years old, he walked 140 miles into Salt Lake City to attend summer school. When
the summer was over, he walked 140 miles home. Even with so little formal
education, he spent virtually his entire adult life as an educator. He was a teacher, a
principal, and even the founder of schools in Utah, Arizona and Mexico.

While in Woodruff, Edward Milo served as Bishop of that small ward. He
wrote many songs and poems-some to his family. To Sarah Elizabeth he penned:
“Sweet is the thoughts of being loved
and loving in return.”

Now, we turn to another family. Wilford Ray and his wife Elsie Margaret
Mortensen had many of the same experiences as the Webb family and other families
who were willing to follow the church leaders and move when asked.

The Ray family was sent to Mexico. They first settled in Arizona in Sunset,
now known as Joseph City. They stayed for awhile and moved on to Sonora,
Mexico. It was in Sonora on June 13, 1891 that Margaret gave birth to a baby girl.
Her name was Della Ray. We came to know her as Grandma Webb.

On June 18, 1898, Edward Milo Webb moved his family to Mexico. It took
two months to travel to Dublan. They moved to Colinia Garcia in 1904 and on to
Colinia Morelos two years later.

Henry worked where he could. At one time, he was in El Paso, Texas working
for a rich railroad man. Here, he learned about steam. He would have stayed, but
his father told him to come home. He was afraid the man in El Paso with lots of
money would not be a good influence for the young Henry. He returned to Mexico
and when he was 17 years old,[handwritten correction above line: unclear]and worked in a sawmill. While at this sawmill, he
became a part of the history of the Southwest.

In 1889, the Apache Kid was convicted of killing a man south of Globe in the
Pinal Mountains. He escaped and fled. Many stories were told of him fleeing into
Mexico. He became a feared outlaw and was know throughout the Southwest. In
1895 or 1896, near the Mormon settlement in western Chihuahua, the Apache Kid
and a small band of followers attacked an outlying ranch and killed a Mrs.
Thompson and her children. Pursuit by the armed ranchers and Mexican soldiers
was unsuccessful, although the pursuers were close enough at times to see the Kid
at a distance.

About a year later, at a ranch in Cave Valley, near the town of Pacheco, the
Indian raiding party returned to steal corn in a field. There were shots exchanged
and the Indians ran off. The next day, the ranchers caught up to them. Most of them
scattered like quail, but the Apache Kid and his wife stood their ground. She had her
papoose on her back during the fight. The Kid was killed and his wife seized the
Kid’s rifle and began to fire at the rancher. She was then killed by a bullet which
also struck her papoose.

The bodies were buried, but the Mexican government later exhumed the bodies
to positively identify the Apache Kid. The bodies were then reburied in very
shallow graves by the Mexican authorities. Those shallow graves caused the young
Henry Webb some exciting moments at the sawmill.

It was Henry’s job to transport the sawdust from the mill to a flat area, some
distance outside the mill. The sawdust was set afire and slowly it would burn. The
wind would blow, causing pot holes all over the flat. One day, to his surprise, Henry
saw a human skull looking up at him from one of the pits. He ran to report his
findings and was told, “Don’t be scared, son. What you just saw was the skull of
either the Apache Kid or his wife. The bones of his child are out there somewhere.”
This story was printed in the Arizona Republic and told by Grandpa Webb to many
of his family and friends.

It was in Colonia Morelos that Della Ray met Henry Webb. After the
prescribed courtship, she agreed to marry him. August 16, 1908, Henry and Della
were married by Bishop Charles Lilly White. On April 12, 1913, they traveled to
Salt Lake City to be sealed in the temple.

They were living in Morelos, Sonora, Mexico when Thora, their first child was
born. This was on September 4, 1909. Two years later on October 19, 1911, Della
gave birth to their first son, Ray.

Page 5

Jobs were scarce in Mexico, so Henry worked where he could. At one time,
he worked at a flourmill which operated until 1951.

When the Mexican Revolution began to pour into the border towns, the
Mormon settlements were affected by the fighting. Several members were
killed,and finally the L.D.S. church authorities and the United States government
ordered the settlers to leave Mexico immediately and to cross the border for their
own protection. They returned to the U.S. on Aug 12, 1912. They found themselves
refugees in their homeland. Many of the settlers had no place to go for help. Some
had families in Utah and others had family in the East. The U.S. government
provided large tents to help the misplaced families. They did what they could, but in
many cases, it was not enough.

The Henry Webb family moved first to Douglas, then on to Tucson. Henry
worked for his half brother Harvey on a dairy ranch in Tucson. Harvey was the son
of Aunt Ellen, Edward Milo’s first wife. Henry also drove a milk truck for Uncle
Frank, Harvey’s brother.

The Ray family moved to a ranch 6 miles west of Tucson where they cleared
brush from the land. From Tucson, the Webb family found their way to the Graham
Mountains, where there were sawmills and tall timber. Thatcher was the birthplace
of their third child, Karl. He was born on January 5, 1914.

Henry found work in the sawmill at Ash Creek, somewhere on the Graham
Mountains. He was a great asset to the sawmill, as he had some knowledge of a
steam mill. He knew how to work in primitive conditions where there were few
supplies and tools.

Henry had a special talent. He had the ability to fix what was broken and to
make something you didn’t have from something you did. Grandma Webb learned
to make a home wherever her house was. She made do with a government tent in
the Graham Mountains.

Many of us have heard Grandpa tell stories of the giant flume constructed
on the north side of Mt. Graham. This was a new experience for Henry as Mt.
Graham was a tough and rugged mountain. The canyons were deep and challenging
to the hard-working lumbermen in those desert mountains. Transportation was one

Page 6

of the most difficult problems the lumbermen faced. Most of the time, the only
solution was to use their trusted wagons and teams of horses.

There were several sawmills built and operated on the Graham Mountains.
From what we know, Henry worked on the mill in Ash Creek. This was one of the
larger mills and it was located in the middle of the timber. It was difficult to move
the logs and lumber on the mountain, so they continued to struggle to find a means
to get the lumber to the valley below. It was decided to build a long slide, or a
flume, down the mountain that would carry both logs and lumber. The flume would
be built with wood, since this was the only material they had and could afford.
Work started in 1924 and was completed in 1925. The flume used for a short time
and then abandoned in 1926. It was called a flume because water flowed in the
trough to reduce friction and not allow the wood to catch on fire. It crossed the deep
canyons and followed the mountain slopes with some of the trestles rising 70 feet
into the air. There are many storied told of men riding logs and lumber down the
flume. One story was about a dead man being strapped to a board and sent sailing
down the mountain to the valley.

From Thatcher, they moved to Pinedale, where sawmills became the center of
their lives in making a living. Pinedale was in the heart of tall pine trees, and due to
transportation problems of logs and lumber, the mills were moved as close to the
trees as possible. The sawmill crew began to take form as another son, Maurice, was
born on September 13, 1916.

The church was fully organized in Pinedale, and since there was always need for
good, willing men, Henry was put to work. He served as counselor to two bishops,
A.J. Brewer and Elija Thomas Jr. He also served as Superintendent of the YMMIA
in the Pinedale Ward.

In 1917, the family moved to Show Low, some 15 miles to the east. It was here
where the Webb’s sawmill family got into a business of their own. Frank Ellsworth
had a small mill on his ranch 1 mile south of town. Henry bought the steam mill and
put it to work. Henry’s sawmill crew began to form as his fourth son, Junius, was
born in Show Low on May 10, 1918.

To be a sawmiller, one of the first things to learn is the talk. It’s important to
know what is a: V-belt, flat belt, feed works, curf, cant hook, choker, tongs, line
shaft, gut chain, wrapper, frozen logs, block setter, and dogger. They had to also
learn a sign language different than any other sign language in use. Many of these

Page 7

words are still used today, as this generation continues to work in the lumber
business and in the logwoods.

The family moved back to Pinedale where Gwen was born in the fall, October
2, 1919. The winters were cold and the snow was very deep. It was difficult to log
and to run a sawmill during those cold winters. February 22, 1923, yet another girl
came into the family. Zola was born in Pinedale. One year later, while still in

Pinedale, another part of the logging crew came into being as Jay was born October
5, 1924.

Since the cold weather continued to cause great difficulty to work during the
winter, Henry decided there may be a better way to spend the winter. In November
1926, the family moved to the Valley so Henry could build houses and provide for
the family. In west Mesa and far east Tempe, where Watson’s now have a flower
shop, there was a medical clinic where Louise was born on March 28, 1927. Thus,
she is the only Webb child born in a birthing facility.

During 1929, the Webb family returned to the mountains to continue the
sawmill business. They moved to Lakeside as their last move, and spent the rest of
their lives in that mountain setting. April 28, 1930, the final crew member, June,
came to the Webb family. He too soon learned what sawdust and hard work were all
about.

In 1936, Henry built the family home by the lake that was to be their home
until their passing. J.H. Webb and Sons became a well- known business name
around the state. They built and operated mills in many locations in the mountains.
There were mills on the Ellsworth Ranch and Pinedale, Vernon, on the ranch in
Lakeside, Mt. Graham, Forest Dale, Canyon Day, Hill Top, and East Fork.

Since sawdust was in the Webb’s bloodstream, Thora married into another
sawmill family — the Petersens. Golden operated a mill south of Pinedale where the
winters were long and cold. He too experienced cold and snowy winters trying to
produce lumber for market.

Sawmills were their livelihood, but their family was the lifeblood of Henry
and Della. With ten children all raised to adulthood, and grandkids all over the
mountain, Henry and Della had a celebration. On August 16, 1957, they celebrated
50 years of marriage. All of their children were present. Thora was married to
Golden Petersen. They had two girls. Ray married Ina Holliday and they had ten
children. Karl married Coral Frost and they were the parents of five children. Reese

Page 8

married Luella Ballard and they had six children. Junius married Verena Fish and
they had two children. Gwen married Clarence Penrod and they had three children.
Zola never married. Jay married Marion Brenay and they had four children. Louise
married Cecil McDowell and they were the parents of four children. June married
Sherry Cluff and they had eight children.

In addition to her own ten children, Grandma helped raise her sister,
Marguerite, and brother, Waldo Ray. Max and Hattie Sadler were the children of
Edna and Max Sadler of the Ray family. Edna passed away and until Max
remarried, Grandma raised Max and Hattie as her own. Louise didn’t know until she
was grown that Waldo wasn’t her brother. She called him Waldo Ray Webb.

Grandpa and Grandma set the standard for service in the church. She wrote in
her life history, “I was active all my life in the service of the church.” She served as
YWMIA president and teacher; Relief Society president and class leader; visiting
teacher (most of her life); Primary president and teacher; and as a Stake missionary
with Grandpa. She also enjoyed working in the temple. Grandma lived by standards
not set by man. Taken from her patriarchal blessing in part, she was given many
promises. "Mother Sarah’s blessings are yours- that your prosperity shall be great.

A righteous branch shall spring from you. These shall be mighty men and women in
the Second Coming of our Redeemer. The Lord delights in you, and will bless you
and your posterity with the blessings of the heaven and earth.”

The children of Henry and Della have responded in service as they knew they
should. They are great people. Twenty-four have served as Stake Clerks, Stake
High Councilors and in Stake Presidencies. Ninety-nine have served as Ward
Clerks, Quorum leaders and in Bishoprics. One hundred thirty nine have filled
missions. Many have served as Stake and Ward organization leaders, teachers and
secretaries.

In closing, it would only be fitting that we honor those of our family who have
passed away. Fred McNeil was the first to pass away. He died on April 4, 1947.
He was the family jokester: He loved to see someone laugh. He passed away as a
young man. He left Aunt Louise a widow at age 19 with two small children to care
for-Kayle and Cheryl.

A.G. Petersen died in 1968 after a very long illnes. He was a man who liked to
make things happen. He had a tender heart when it came to his two daughters and
his wife Thora. His grandkids were the love of his life. He was a hard worker and
considered one of the better sawmillers on the mountain.

Page 9

Thora was the big sister to the Webb clan. She was a great cook and loved to
sew for her girls and everyone else, but never herself. She sang beautifully and
would dance whenever she could. As others, her grandkids made her life complete.
Her great disappointment was only being able to hold her first great grandchild one
time before she passed away in 1974.

Luella passed away on March 30, 1977. She was the “Bell of the Ball”. Her
life was full of service to others. Until the time of her passing, she always was
willing to sew burial clothes for many throughout northern Arizona. She taught her
children to be the very best, and it shows. After her passing, Uncle Reese married
another jewel, Cheryl.

Junius was the quiet one. He never said a bad word about anyone. He was a
hard worker who never complained of those difficult times in life. Completely
dependable in every aspect of his life, he served 25 years as clerk in the church,
before passing away July 15, 1985.

Clarence, or Penrod as he was affectionately called, was loved for his infectious
laugh. When he was around, there was never a dull moment. He loved his family.
Lance was his pride and joy. He loved to watch him compete in sports at all levels.
He loved his grandchildren and they knew it. He passed away in 1986.

Zola was the California girl. She spent many years living on the Coast. Her
home was always open and she loved to have everyone come to see her. She loved
horses, and at one time, wanted to be a trick rider. She never married and only had
one love, Charles Porterfield. Her beautiful hair was her trademark.

On a beautiful winter day, Feb. 20, 1999, the Webb family and friends gathered
at the Lakeside Stake Center to honor the passing of their loved one — Henry Ray
Webb. Ray was the peacemaker of the family and was loved by all who knew him.
He passed away Feb. 18, 1999 in Mesa, Arizona, where he was being cared for ina
nursing home. He was buried in Lakeside, among the tall pines which he loved so
much. He was an LDS Bishop and served all of his life in the church. He loved his
family and served them throughout his life.

Eight grandchildren, or the spouses of grandchildren, have also passed away, as
well as two great-grandchildren.

We close with a tribute to Grandpa and Grandma Webb written by their great-
granddaughter Dori Thompson.

## Evidence qualifications

Sonora birth location differs from the civil Pacheco, Chihuahua record. April 12, 1913 sealing is retrospective and conflicts with April 2 in the family book; the surviving April 7 application does not establish a sealing date. The printed 1957 fiftieth anniversary differs from the contemporary 1958 invitation. Jay and Marion had six children, not the four listed here. Statements about earlier Webb generations, the Apache Kid and flume histories are family narrative, not independently proved events.
