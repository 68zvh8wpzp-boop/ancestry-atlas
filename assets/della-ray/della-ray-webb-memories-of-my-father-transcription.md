## Original transcription — page 1

Memories of My Father

As I remember Father as a child, he was a wonderful father to me. As I
grew older he was more wonderful. Father was loving and kind to us, but at
the same time he was stern with us children. When he told us to do something,
we knew he meant it.

He moved from Utah to Sunset as it was called then (a place between
Winslow and Joseph City, Arizona). They lived in the United Order. Then he
took his families and moved to Colonia Diaz, Chihuahua, Mexico. There our
oldest sister, Edna, was born January 13, 1887 - the first white child that
was born in Colonia Diaz. My brother, John Alexander was born Nov.11,1988 here.

Father pioneered all of his life. He then moved to Pacheco, Chichuahua,
Mexico. I was born here the 13th day of June 1891. Then before I was a
year old, he moved to Colonia Oaxaca, Sonra, Mexico. He took a farm on the
Bavispi River. They raised all kinds of vegetables and grains. We used to
grate potatoes to make starch. Father was a good provider. We always had
plenty of milk, butter and cheese. They made their-own butter and cheese.

When we were small children at Oaxaca, father worked quite a bit at a
Ranch between Colonia Juarez and Oaxaca. He came home just before christmas
as I remember.

We always knew when Father was coming home. He always blew his nose
horn as we called it. It had quite a whistle to it. He had a Santa Clause
mask on and us little ones had never seen one before, we all broke and ran
because we were scared to death. Father brought Ada and me a set of little
china dishes. The cups just fit our finger like a thimble. We thought they
were the cutest things we ever saw.

When I was about ten years old, they were settling another town about
28 miles below Oaxaca. They called it Colonia Morelos, Sonora, Mexico. We
moved there.

The first thing Father did when we moved to a place was dig a well so
that we could have water. There he took up new farms and went to farming
again.

All of us children that were big enough would help harvest potatoes,
peanuts, and strip cane to make molasses. We would make swords out of
mesquite or cotton wood limbs to strip the cane. We would cut all the leaves
off then they would cut the cane and haul it to the mill. They would grind
all the juice out of the cane and put it in bug vats and cook it until it
was a thick syrup. Father made some of the best molasses. The last batch he
made, he would leave some of it in the vat then he would put green corn in it
and cook it to make too. He would invite the whole town and what a jolly time
candy [displaced beneath “too.”]
we would have.

Our farm was about four miles from town. Everybody raised watermellons.
What a time we would have on hay rack rides with watermellon busts. The farms
were all along the Bavispi River in Colonia Morelos.

In the year about 1905, there was a cloud burst up above Oaxaca. It
washed the town all away in Oaxaca except about two houses the the school
house. Everyone got out without loosing a life. There were pigs, chickens on

the roof tops as they came floating by. They all stayed in the school house.
By the time it got down to Morelos, it had calmed down some, but it washed
all the farms away and just left big sand mounds. It changed the course of
the river bed. It left our home right on the bank. They had to secure the
bank with barbed wire and cotton wood limbs.

Father had many friends among the Mexican people. He could rattle the
Maexican language off like they did. They called him Santiego Rey.

After the farms were all washed away, they all took farms higher up on
the Bavispi Creek. There they raised wheat and oats. We girls, Chloe, Ada,
and I would go with Father while they thrashed the wheat and cook for the
thrashers. on a campfire a week at a time. We all enjoyed it.

Father had a penetrating voice. He would just talk in his natural way
and you could hear it way off. One day Chloe and I were up town when he
came from work. He said, "Oh Chloe, Come open the gate." We heard him up
town.

Father never held feelings. He always forgave anyone of their mistakes.

In 1912, the Mexican War broke out and out Church authorities and the U.S.
Government advised us to leave. We left everything we owned behind and never
went back. The U.S. Government furnished tents and some provisions for all.
They pitched the tents like a town. The People would come out from Douglas
in crouds and go up and down the rows of tents to see what they could see.

Father and Ada were Standing out by the tent when some people came by and
Ada grabbed a handfull of dirt and threw on them. They acted like we were a
circus. We were all Mormons. I guess they thought they would see horns on
us as they believed the Mormon people had. I had been married almost four
years at this time.

Just before we left, there was a special priesthood meeting held. Bishop
Charles W. Lillywhite asked some of the brethern to stay. a special prayer was
offered and asked for protection. After getting out of town, we could see the
red army on the other side of the Bavispi Creek. There hadn't been any storm
but the Bavispi Creek was running full from bank to bank. So the red army
turned and left. That is the way the Lord protected us.

We left Mexico August 12, 1912; landed in Douglas, then went to Tucson
and stayed there for awhile. Father went to Chandler and bought a farm.

He was a lover of horses. He had some of the most beautiful horses you
ever saw. After they went to Chandler, he would hitch his pretty horses to
his buggy and put the smaller children in the buggy and away they would go
to the state fair in Phoenix.

After we left Tucson, I never saw much of father. I was married and had
two children - Thora and Ray. When they were little, they used to stand by
Father's knees and sing to him. They would sing "Jesus Wants Me For A
Sunbeam" and "Hark, Listen To The Classmate Song". We went to the mountains
to a little place called pinedale, near Snowflake, Arizona.

Father would be happy if he could see his posterity now.

Della Ray Webb
January 1964

## Evidence qualifications

The document explicitly dates its account January 1964. Its John Alexander birth year “1988” is an obvious chronological defect, retained rather than silently corrected. Edna's claimed first-white-child status is family testimony and not established independently. “Mexican War” beginning in 1912 is the recollection's wording; it does not date the beginning of the Mexican Revolution. Della's “about ten” age for the Morelos move differs from the later edited story's age eleven. Neither supplies a proved exact move year. Flood date “about 1905,” total destruction and no-loss-of-life claims require contemporary correlation. Religious interpretation and “red army” identification are reproduced as her account, without historical verification. Her stated 12 August 1912 departure agrees with the separate handwritten life sketch, but these are accounts by the same witness, not two independent contemporary sources.
