# Wife No. 19 — a source-faithful Atlas supplement

**Edition:** 1876 illustrated edition (copyright 1875); 41 chapters; 605 numbered pages

> Ann Eliza Webb Young’s Wife No. 19 (1875–76) is more than a memoir of marriage to Brigham Young. In 41 chapters she traces her parents’ conversion, her childhood in a plural family, the migration to Utah, her first marriage and two sons, a later union she says was pressed upon her, exhausting work and neglect, and her decision to leave and speak publicly against polygamy. The book is both personal testimony and a forceful reform-era polemic; its claims about violence and other people need independent checking.

**How to read this:** This is an authored public advocacy memoir, NOT a neutral verified chronicle. Chapters I–IV contain inherited family history; many allegations about other people in IX–XVI and XXX–XXXIX were not eyewitnessed. The preserved scanned-volume OCR is uncorrected. Dates of marriage disagree within the 1876 book and between institutional records. A separate afterword covers later life not in the original 1876 text.

## 1. The testimony she wanted to leave
*Original source: Preface and dedication; Chapters I–IV*

Young dedicates the book to women still living in plural households in Utah. She presents herself not as a neutral chronicler but as a former believer addressing women whose fears, loyalties and sacrifices she says she understood. The subtitle promises an exposure of Mormonism and the suffering of women in polygamy; contemporary readers would have recognized the language of nineteenth-century reform and religious controversy. The introductory endorsements from John B. Gough and Mary A. Livermore position her account within public moral advocacy.

The opening chapters look backward before her own birth. She reconstructs how her parents and their circle joined Joseph Smith’s movement, the hopes that drew converts to Kirtland and Nauvoo, repeated dislocation and loss, Smith’s death, and Brigham Young’s succession. Because Ann Eliza was born in Nauvoo in September 1844, much of this early history is family recollection or the author’s later interpretation rather than events she personally witnessed. Her parents, Chauncey Griswold Webb and Eliza Jane Churchill, are central to the account: they begin as believers willing to sacrifice comfort and property for their faith.

## 2. A girl grows up inside a plural family
*Original source: Chapters V–VIII*

Her childhood story turns on the emotional cost of plural marriage in her own home. Young portrays her mother as having accepted a teaching she dreaded, fearing that resistance to church leadership was resistance to God. The first additional wife, Elizabeth Taft, is not written as an uncomplicated villain: Ann Eliza describes her kindness to the children and the affection the family initially felt for her. That complexity matters, because the book’s grievance is against the system of unequal power, not simply against one rival woman.

She recounts the Nauvoo sealing arrangement in early 1846, with her mother acknowledging a second wife, and describes how ordinary domestic tasks, invitations, affection and rank became charged with pain. Her depictions of her mother’s despondency are intensely personal but also include conversations reported to Ann Eliza afterward. The family’s westward journey and early Utah years supply a second landscape: the difficulty of settlement, food shortages, illness, home-making and communal work. The author places these memories next to accounts of leadership privilege, so that the contrast between ordinary women’s labor and influential men’s prerogatives becomes an organizing theme.

## 3. From private suffering to a critique of authority
*Original source: Chapters IX–XVI*

Young broadens her account to Utah public life. She criticizes missionary finance, migration schemes, the handcart system, church economic power, and the unequal treatment of converts and women. Her narrative of the 1856–57 Utah Reformation describes compulsory public confession, rebaptism, surveillance and preaching about blood atonement as she understood them. She also retells the Mountain Meadows Massacre and reports of killings or intimidation, including accounts attributed to others.

This part of the book requires particularly careful reading. Her reports of events such as the massacre and alleged secret killings are presented as accusations in an avowed anti-polygamy exposé; many are not scenes she witnessed herself. They should neither be silently treated as proven eyewitness evidence nor omitted from a faithful account of what she tried to expose. For Ann Eliza, these chapters explain why she came to regard the church hierarchy as able to control both household decisions and public conduct, and why she later feared the consequences of dissent.

## 4. Her father’s expanding household and a family under strain
*Original source: Chapters XVII–XXI*

The story returns repeatedly to the Webb household. Ann Eliza describes her father taking additional wives, younger women entering the family, her mother trying to accommodate complicated relationships, and the conflict surrounding Louise. She sometimes writes sharply or ironically about particular people but also shows a shared predicament: women under pressure to consent, men influenced by leadership counsel, and affection or resentment changing as a family grows. The result is not a simple chronology of marriages; it is an account of uncertainty inside an ostensibly sanctified household.

She links the family’s hardship to money and church influence. A striking thread concerns Chauncey’s work and the telegraph-pole contracts undertaken by her brother Gilbert, which she says left him heavily indebted and entangled with men close to Brigham Young. Her portrayal of this financial dispute is later essential to how she explains the pressure placed upon her to accept Young’s proposal. It is firsthand family advocacy about an economic disagreement, not a substitute for the underlying contracts or accounts.

## 5. A young woman, a first marriage, and two children
*Original source: Chapters XXII–XXVI*

Before she became associated publicly with Brigham Young, Ann Eliza was a young woman making decisions amid a society in which she felt religious leaders interfered in courtship. She describes life around the Salt Lake theater and the Lion House, then her marriage to James Leech Dee. The marriage yielded two sons. In her telling, it deteriorated through jealousy, threats that Dee might take another wife, violence and a widening gulf between her expectations and her husband’s conduct.

She portrays her mother as a refuge and her first divorce as an escape from a painful and unsafe relationship. The narrative is important because Ann Eliza did not approach her subsequent union with Brigham Young as an inexperienced bride: she emphasizes being a mother responsible for two children and having already decided against another marriage. Her book repeatedly returns to that refusal to explain why she saw Young’s pursuit as coercion rather than romantic courtship.

## 6. Why she says she agreed to marry Brigham Young
*Original source: Chapters XXVII–XXIX*

Ann Eliza says that when Young proposed, she was horrified at the prospect of marrying a man far older than herself and already responsible for many households. Her parents viewed an offer from the church’s president differently, seeing social or religious honor as well as possible danger in refusal. The account reproduces her insistence that the decision should be hers and her frustration that spiritual duty could be invoked against her judgment.

The turning point in her version is not sudden affection: it is pressure extending into her family’s economic survival. She describes Gilbert’s unpaid telegraph work, debt, and the threat of disfellowshipment. She believed refusal would place her relatives at greater risk. Young’s proposal promised a house and financial allowance, while the ceremony itself, in her account, was kept quiet for fear of publicity and objections from other wives. Her narration distinguishes outward compliance from willing consent. This is her own explanation, not an adjudicated finding about Young’s intentions.

## 7. The ceremony’s disputed date belongs in the record
*Original source: Chapters XXIX and XXXVII; compared with institutional chronology*

The text of the 1876 edition is not perfectly consistent about even this central date. Chapter XXIX says the Endowment House ceremony occurred on 7 April 1869. But in Chapter XXXVII, while summarizing the allegations made in her 1873 court complaint, she says 6 April 1868. Church Historians Press dates the marriage 7 April 1868; Utah State Archives also reports April 1868; BYU Special Collections instead gives 7 April 1869. An institutional Church History profile even records a May 1868 date. These are competing textual and compiled claims, not a reason to invent certainty.

The Atlas therefore treats the specific day and year as disputed and anchors its narrative to the sequence that is established: after the divorce from Dee and before the separation and 1873 litigation. The memoir’s famous title is another point where rhetoric should not be mistaken for a verified statistic: 'No. 19' is the author’s public designation, not a definitive count of Young’s plural marriages.

## 8. Marriage to a powerful man did not bring the security promised
*Original source: Chapters XXIX–XXXV*

In chapters about Young’s household, his other wives and children, Ann Eliza observes private relationships, the distribution of food and money, the position of wives according to perceived favor, and the contrast between public authority and domestic dependence. She records grievances involving the Lion House and households elsewhere, but also gives portraits of named women whose histories were already marked by children, illness, distance or abandonment. Her tone shifts between intimate observation, biting satire, rumor about others and arguments against the entire plural system.

She says Young moved her and her mother to a farm roughly four miles from Salt Lake City after about a year. In Chapter XXXV, she describes dairy work involving forty cows and meals for roughly twenty-five to thirty workers, under uncomfortable living conditions. She says the work harmed their health, that promised support failed to materialize, and that even after moving back to a city house she took boarders to sustain herself and her sons. An everyday request for a better cooking stove became, in her telling, a revealing exchange in which Young refused to help and expected her to provide for herself.

## 9. Breaking with the marriage and with the faith
*Original source: Chapters XXXV–XXXVI*

Ann Eliza traces the change from private discontent to a deliberate break. Her conversations with non-Mormon friends and boarders, including the Strattons and attorney Hagan, gave her confidence that another life was possible. She increasingly separated her belief in God from obedience to Young and his church. Her mother, still attached to the faith, could not simply make the same break at the same time.

She narrates refuge at the Walker House in Salt Lake City, fear of surveillance and retaliation, advice to seek legal relief, newspaper curiosity and the emotional cost of being treated as an object of scandal. The book’s phrase 'breaking the yoke' describes her chosen exit from what she understood as a binding relationship and religious authority. Her later public designation as an apostate and the church’s excommunication in 1874 must be distinguished from being officially 'sent into exile'; the memoir describes an oppressive farm assignment followed by her own departure, not an independently verified formal exile order.

## 10. A divorce case that tested whether the union existed in law
*Original source: Chapter XXXVII; later court record external to the book*

Her reproduction of the lawsuit makes money, law and personhood inseparable. She filed a complaint in July 1873 alleging neglect, harsh treatment and insufficient support for herself and two children. She described her work at the farm, her health, the sale of household belongings, and the large disparity she believed existed between Young’s wealth and her circumstances. She sought separation, alimony and legal fees. Those claims are her sworn allegations and should be recognized as such, not converted into an unchallenged judgment of fact.

Young’s answer, as she reproduces it, denied the existence of a legally recognized marriage even while acknowledging a religious ceremony. She follows shifting jurisdictional rulings, hearings, affidavits and the difficulty of establishing a wife’s rights where a plural union was legally contested. The 1876 book could only describe the proceedings known at its composition; the subsequent April 1877 ruling reported by Church Historians Press treated the union as legally invalid and rejected divorce and alimony under that basis. The 1875 trial-stage language reported by Utah State Archives cannot be collapsed into a single uncontested end date.

## 11. From threatened dissenter to author and lecturer
*Original source: Chapters XXXVIII–XXXIX*

Young says she first addressed sympathetic listeners at the Walker House, then reluctantly accepted encouragement to speak publicly. She describes leaving Salt Lake City covertly at night in late November, traveling with a female companion, and fearing pursuit. At the same time she portrays her father as supportive, making her family story more complicated than an account of simple estrangement from every believer. She began speaking in Denver, and later addressed audiences farther east, including Boston and Washington.

She repeatedly rejects the accusation that she lectured merely for profit or notoriety. Instead she presents public speech as an obligation to women left behind. The book also turns into a wide-ranging critique of church government, business institutions and collective economic schemes, including Zion’s Cooperative Mercantile Institution and the Order of Enoch. For historians, that combination is the book’s character: part memoir, part contemporary polemic, part political appeal. In 1874 she also testified before Congress about polygamy; her testimony and legal actions require sources outside her memoir for their official details.

[Read Chapter 38](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Wife_No._19/Chapter_38)


## 12. Her ending is not simply departure—it is a contested love of Utah
*Original source: Chapters XL–XLI, final pages*

The final chapters catalogue the inequalities Young believed plural marriage produced among both affluent and poor women. She writes with sweeping certainty about women’s unhappiness, an argument drawn from experience, reported cases and a reform campaign rather than a statistically representative study. She calls on women and readers outside Utah to confront a system that, in her view, made wives economically dependent and deprived them of meaningful choice.

Yet the close is emotionally subtler than simple repudiation. She describes returning to Salt Lake City in August 1874 to lecture, meeting old friends, seeing her father and mother in an audience, and recognizing that Utah remained home to her even after her break with its dominant religious institutions. She ends by anticipating a future in which the place and the people she cared for would be freer. A faithful synopsis should retain that affection and grief alongside anger, allegations and political determination.

## Afterword — what happened after this 1876 narrative
*Original source: Independent archival chronology; not part of the 1876 memoir*

Utah State Archives records that Ann Eliza was excommunicated in 1874 and later joined the Methodist tradition. Church Historians Press documents her continued public campaigning through the early 1880s and an additional marriage to Moses R. Deming in 1883, which also ended. She issued a revised version of her memoir in 1908 and was living in Sparks, Nevada, by 1910. She died in 1917. Institutional sources differ over the exact December death day, so no contested day is asserted here as beyond doubt.

Her historical significance does not depend on being a numbered wife. She was a Webb daughter who published a lengthy account of her household’s migration and private costs, publicly challenged a powerful religious leader, used courts and public lectures to contest her treatment, and became a recognizable author and opponent of polygamy. The original 1876 text preserves her voice; independent records remain essential for separating that voice’s verifiable personal details, family memories, attributed speech and sensational charges.
