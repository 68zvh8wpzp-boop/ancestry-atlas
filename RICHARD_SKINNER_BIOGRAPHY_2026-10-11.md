Richard Francis Skinner was born on 11 November 1862 in Greenwood Township, Michigan, during the Civil War. His parents were John Skinner and Marian Lewis, whose name also appears as Marion and Mary. John had been born in Canada; Marian was born in Michigan. Richard grew up in a family whose homes and livelihoods crossed several western Michigan townships before his own work carried him farther north and eventually to Muskegon.

Michigan had been a state for twenty-five years when Richard was born. Greenwood Township had been organized only four years earlier, and Oceana County was still developing its roads, farms and local institutions. Lake Michigan, inland streams and the forests connected the county to a larger economy. Sawmills supplied lumber for growing Midwestern cities, while cleared land supported farms. This was also Odawa and Anishinaabe country, with a history extending far beyond the new township boundaries. Odawa communities continued in nearby Elbridge and Crystal townships as treaties and settlement changed control of the land.

Before Richard’s birth, John and Mary lived in Polkton Township in Ottawa County with Priscilla, Frederick and a baby entered as Mary E. By September 1870, eight-year-old Richard was living with John and Marion in Denver Township, Newaygo County, in the Hesperia area. John farmed, and thirteen-year-old Frederick already worked as a farm laborer. Mariette, Richard and little William completed the household. Richard’s school attendance was marked that year. The family’s property was valued at $800 in real estate and $200 in personal possessions.

In June 1880 the family lived in Leavitt Township, Oceana County. John remained a farmer, while Marian kept house. Seventeen-year-old Richard was single and worked “by day,” an early indication of the wage labor that would recur throughout his life. His younger brothers William and John were still at home. The household also included his married sister Priscilla Carlton and her children William P. and Claude P. Three generations shared this home, with Richard listed explicitly as John’s son.

Richard married Ellen Marie Hay on 17 September 1882 at Newfield. He was nineteen and a farmer; Ellen was seventeen. Both lived in Newfield and had been born in Michigan. T. C. Landon, a justice, performed the marriage, with J. E. Landon and George, whose surname is difficult to read, as witnesses. Richard’s first marriage began within the western Michigan communities where his family had already spent many years.

Richard and Ellen’s children were Millie Irene and Marcie Leo. Millie was born in November 1889. Their son’s birth return places him at Torch Lake in Antrim County on 20 January 1894, entered under the spelling Marcia Skinner. Richard was then a laborer, and both parents lived at Torch Lake. Other later accounts give a different year for Marcie’s birth, but his place in Richard and Ellen’s family is clear. Ellen died in 1899, leaving Richard with two young children.

On 5 November 1899 Richard married Jessie Irene Aurin in Elbridge Township. They had obtained their license two days earlier. Richard was thirty-six, a farmer living in Leavitt; Jessie was twenty and lived in Elbridge. This was Richard’s second marriage and Jessie’s first. Richard named John Skinner and Marion Lewis as his parents. Hattie Thornton was one of the witnesses to the wedding, which joined Jessie to a household that already included Millie and Marcie.

By June 1900 Richard, Jessie, ten-year-old Millie and six-year-old Marcie lived together in Leavitt Township. Richard’s farm was owned with a mortgage. Millie attended school, and the children were identified as Richard’s daughter and son. Clifford D., the first of Richard and Jessie’s children, was born that December at Colfax. His birth return carries later corrections identifying his parents as Richard Francis Skinner and Jessie Aurin. Richard was still described as a farmer.

Richard and Jessie’s children were Clifford D., Marian Beulah, Ellen Madelia, John Andrew, Richard Francis and Amanda Melvina. The younger Richard Francis died in infancy in 1910. Amanda, the youngest, was born in 1918, when her oldest brother Clifford was approaching adulthood. The wide age range meant that Richard’s home held both working sons and very young children during his later middle years.

In January 1920 the family lived in Briley Township, Montmorency County. Richard, fifty-eight, worked as a laborer in the lumber woods. Marcie and Clifford worked in a lumber camp. Marian, Madelia and John were still at home, together with Amanda, only a little over a year old. The household also included an eighty-year-old widow, Mary Shoemaker, entered as Richard’s mother. Her identity and surname remain unresolved. Richard and Jessie owned their home with a mortgage, continuing the combination of household ownership and wage work visible in his earlier years.

That September, seventeen-year-old Marian married Albert Brenay in Alpena. Albert was a Canadian-born farmer living at Hubbard Lake; Marian lived at Atlanta. In March 1923 Madelia married James King, another Atlanta resident, in Alpena. Their marriages connected the Skinner household to families beyond Richard’s childhood communities. Marian and Albert became the parents of Marion Beulah Brenay, through whom Richard’s family joined the later Webb generations.

By April 1930 Richard lived in Muskegon with Clifford and a nineteen-year-old cousin, Gladden N. Skinner. Richard and Clifford both worked as laborers in a flour mill. Their home rented for $15 a month. Clifford was recorded as out of work at the time of the enumeration, while Richard was working. This household belonged to the opening years of the Great Depression, in a Lake Michigan city whose manufacturing and shipping economy had grown from its earlier lumber trade.

Richard died on 17 November 1933 in South Branch Township, Crawford County, six days after his seventy-first birthday. His usual address was 2063 Estes Street in Muskegon, although he had been in the place where he died for four years. Clifford supplied the information for his death registration. Richard’s occupation was day labor, with fifty years entered for his working life. The cause of death was acute dilatation of the heart. He was buried at Grayling on 20 November.

Richard’s family continued to move beyond Michigan. In September 1936 Amanda married Walter Dan English in Salt Lake City, with her sister Marian B. Brenay among the witnesses. Across Richard’s lifetime, farming, day labor, lumber work and mill employment sustained a family that grew through two marriages. His children’s marriages and successive homes carried those relationships from Michigan’s townships into a much wider family geography.
