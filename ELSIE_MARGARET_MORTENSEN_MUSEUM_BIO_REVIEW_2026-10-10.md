# Elsie Margaret Mortensen Ray — museum biography and regional setting

## Biography

Elsie Margaret Mortensen was born on 16 August 1864 in Gunnison, Utah Territory, the daughter of Danish-born immigrants Morten Peder Mortensen and Dorothea Knudsen Jensen. She grew up in a household where work on the land and care for animals shaped the days. The family raised grain, vegetables and fruit, kept sheep, and spun or worked wool for clothing. The routines of milking, food preparation and sewing were part of a childhood in which a family's needs had to be met largely through its own labor.

When Margaret was twelve, her parents joined a movement from Utah into Arizona Territory. They set out on 28 October 1876, taking their possessions and livestock over difficult roads toward settlements along the Little Colorado River. Her adolescence brought both practical duties and occasions for recreation. Family memories describe young people moving benches aside after supper to square-dance to fiddles and banjos, playing games and pulling molasses candy. Such evenings belonged to the same household world as the day's demanding chores.

Margaret had already known James Wilford Ray when he worked for her family in Utah. Their paths crossed again in Arizona, and they married at St. George, Utah, in January 1882. Their first son, James Wilford Jr., arrived in 1885. James also married Margaret's younger sister Maria Helena Mortensen in 1884, establishing a plural-marriage family. The sisters initially shared a home in the Mexican colonies, then maintained separate households while their children remained connected. Margaret and Maria each had responsibilities for her own children, and family recollections describe cooperation between them.

In the mid-1880s, Margaret joined her parents and James in moving into northern Mexico. Their growing household lived at Colonia Díaz, then in the mountain settlement of Pacheco, where Della was born in 1891. They subsequently lived at Oaxaca, in Sonora, where work and water were closely tied to agriculture. Children were born into successive homes, and daughters and sons grew up among a broad network of siblings, half-siblings and nearby relatives.

In 1900, when the family was preparing another move, Margaret stayed at Oaxaca until her children completed the school term. She then took them to Colonia Morelos. Brick homes, local shops and established schools offered more settled surroundings than some earlier locations, although farming and maintaining a large household still required steady work. By the birth of her youngest son, Waldo, in 1909, Margaret had given birth to twelve children: James Wilford Jr., Edna, John Alexander, Della, Sims Flood, Milo Eugene, Dorothy, Ferneth Evelena, Elsie, Clifton, Marguerite and Waldo. Dorothy and Clifton had died in early childhood.

The upheaval of the Mexican Revolution brought another wrenching move. In 1912, Margaret and her household left the colonies for the United States, abandoning the hope of permanent settlement there. They received temporary assistance after crossing the border, while James and the older sons sought work in the Tucson area. The return to Arizona took place just as several of the older children were beginning lives of their own. Margaret still had younger children to raise, but fewer older hands remained in her home to help with the work.

In the autumn of 1914 the Rays settled near Chandler, where James acquired an eighty-acre farm. Margaret lived in a house toward one end of the property, while Maria Helena's home stood roughly a quarter-mile away. Their houses were linked by the road later known as Ray Road. It was a return to agricultural life in a new part of Arizona, but Margaret's health had worsened in the years following Waldo's birth, and she could no longer take part as fully in the work she had once managed.

After medical treatment for gallstones, Margaret underwent an operation in 1918. She did not recover. Family recollections remember a difficult final illness and care provided by her niece Ada, who had trained as a nurse. Margaret died in June 1918, aged fifty-three. She was buried at Mesa Cemetery on 24 June, close to relatives whose lives had been intertwined with her own.

Her life crossed Utah, Arizona and Mexico, and involved repeated efforts to establish and sustain a home under very different conditions. The twelve children she bore, the work of raising them, and the continued ties among her extended family gave that life a reach far beyond the settlements in which she had lived.

## Historical setting — distinct from biography

**Utah Territory and the Little Colorado settlements, 1860s–1880s.** Mormon communities in central Utah relied heavily on irrigated farming, animal husbandry, domestic textile work and household production. The settlement movement into Arizona Territory crossed long-established Indigenous homelands. Communities along the Little Colorado developed around agriculture and transportation routes amid the difficulties of distance, climate and limited infrastructure.

**Northern Mexican colonies, 1880s–1912.** Colonia Díaz, Colonia Pacheco, Colonia Oaxaca and Colonia Morelos were among settlements established by American Latter-day Saint families in northern Mexico. Their histories unfolded within Mexican sovereignty and existing Indigenous and local Mexican communities. Houses, farming and schools developed unevenly from one colony to another. Armed upheaval during the Mexican Revolution led to a large-scale departure in 1912.

**Chandler and the Salt River Valley, 1912–1918.** Irrigation development and land cultivation transformed the Salt River Valley as newly arriving families searched for agricultural opportunities. Communities that had been largely rural were linked increasingly by roads, freight and regional commerce. Medical treatment was available but operations remained serious undertakings, especially for patients whose health was already poor.
